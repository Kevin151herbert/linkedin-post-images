A Refund Rule Is Also a Price List

Mandatory fraud refunds protect real victims. They also turn your fraud investigator into the last control standing between a convincing story and your P&L.

Every new fraud refund rule gets the same reception. It's a win for customers. Banks will finally have skin in the game. Fraud prevention budgets will go up. All of that is true, and I'm glad of it. People who are tricked out of their savings deserve a fast, fair answer.

But there's a second reading of every refund rule that rarely makes the press release. A rule that says "if you were deceived, we will pay you back" also tells a determined fraudster exactly what a successful claim has to look like.

The question isn't whether refunds are good. It's whether your organisation can tell the difference between a victim and an accomplice who has learned to sound like one.

The case that taught me this

At a fast-growing Nigerian neobank, a pattern of transaction fraud emerged while I was on vacation. The analyst in charge at the time read it the way most of us would: ordinary fraud, caused by a lack of controls. Customers' accounts had been "hacked".

I took a different route and went to the data for every affected customer: over 80,000 transactions. One detail didn't fit the story. There was never a password change on the affected accounts. There was only a device switch, a new device logging in.

Those new-device logins traced back to just five unique devices. And those devices traced back to the original accounts they belonged to.

It wasn't account takeover. Customers were colluding with the fraudster. They handed over their login credentials, the fraudster drew overdrafts on their accounts and took the money, the customers reported their accounts as hacked, the bank wrote off the overdrafts as losses, and the customers split the proceeds with the fraudster.

The label "normal fraud, weak controls" was wrong. The data told a different story the moment someone recreated the scheme end to end. Every breach tells a story. Collusion fraud is simply a story written to be misread.

What the new rules actually say

The Central Bank of Nigeria's exposure draft of the Guidelines for Handling Authorised Push Payment Fraud (November 2025) is a serious piece of consumer protection. Among other things, it proposes:

  • Reporting channels open 24 hours a day, seven days a week, with acknowledgement and a case reference within 24 hours.

  • A full investigation concluded within 14 working days, and reimbursement within 48 hours of a documented investigation concluding.

  • Where neither the sending nor the receiving institution is at fault and the customer is eligible, the institutions reimburse the customer with the cost shared equally.

  • Eligibility only where there is "no evidence of negligence, collusion, or criminal intent" on the customer's part, and exclusions where the customer acted fraudulently or with negligence.

  • Reasons must be given for any denial, and unresolved complaints escalate to the CBN's Consumer Protection and Financial Inclusion Department.

Europe is travelling in the same direction. The EU's new Payment Services Regulation, politically agreed in late 2025 and still awaiting formal publication, will require providers to refund customers manipulated by fraudsters impersonating their bank, unless the customer acted fraudulently or with gross negligence. The UK has run a mandatory reimbursement regime for authorised push payment scams since October 2024.

The missed angle: the burden moves to evidence

Most commentary reads these rules as a story about speed: faster acknowledgement, faster investigation, faster money back. The part that matters more for a risk function is quieter.

Read the eligibility test again. The customer is refunded where there is no evidence of collusion. In practice, that means the bank's position is only as strong as the evidence it can assemble, explain and defend within 14 working days, in a decision that must give reasons and can be escalated to the regulator.

That changes three things, and none of them is about customer service.

🔴 Collusion is designed to look like victimhood. A colluding customer reports promptly, has every detail ready and cooperates fully, because the claim is the product. A genuinely deceived, elderly or panicked customer may report late, remember little and contradict themselves. A process that rewards a tidy, fast report can end up screening in the wrong direction. The draft's higher duty of care for vulnerable customers helps, but only if investigators know what they are looking for.

🔴 The 50/50 split spreads the cost of what nobody saw. In a no-fault case, the receiving institution pays half for a scheme it may have had no way to detect on its own. That is a powerful incentive for institutions to share data quickly (the draft asks the originating institution to notify the others within 30 minutes, in line with the Nigeria Data Protection Act). It is also a reason to make sure your own house is the one with the evidence.

🔴 Refund certainty is an incentive, not just a safeguard. Once a payout is predictable, recruiting account holders into a "you report it, we split it" arrangement becomes a business model. Not because customers are bad, but because rational actors respond to prices, and a refund rule publishes one.

What this means for you

None of this is an argument against refunds. Treating every claimant as a suspect would punish real victims and destroy the trust these rules exist to build. The point is that the investigation function has quietly become a control on the balance sheet, and it should be resourced like one.

🟢 Make your login and device trail queryable in hours, not weeks. In my case, the decisive signal was sitting in data the bank already had. It only mattered because someone looked at all of it, not a sample.

🟢 Train investigators to rebuild the scheme, not process the claim. Ask who benefits, how the money moved and what the "victim" would have to have done for the story to be false. Then check.

🟢 Feed every closed case back into product design. Once you understand one scheme end to end, you can see the other products exposed to the same loophole and close them before the next wave.

❌ Stop closing cases as "isolated incidents". "It only affected a few customers" is how patterns stay invisible.

❌ Stop pricing refunds as a cost of doing business. A loss line you have accepted is a loss line nobody investigates.

Before these guidelines are finalised, three questions are worth putting to your board or executive committee:

  • Evidence: Could we tell a victim from an accomplice, using data we already capture, within 14 working days?

  • Ownership: Who decides to deny a refund, and would that decision and its reasons survive escalation to the CBN?

  • Learning: When a fraud case closes, who is responsible for asking which other products share the same weakness?

If twenty "I was scammed" claims landed on our desk tomorrow, how many would we pay simply because we couldn't prove otherwise?

Frameworks tell you what to check. They don't tell you how to think. The new refund rules will reward the organisations whose people can think like the person on the other side of the claim.

A refund rule is also a price list. Someone will read it that way.
